Who this guide is for

Start here, before recommending.

Practitioners with a Practitioner Hub account who are recommending Daniel's Diaries to a family with an NDIS plan. It explains where the subscription can sit within a plan, what plan managers look for, and how to use the recommendation letter template so a family's claim is as clean as possible. It is written to be shared: you can talk a parent through it in five minutes, or hand the relevant section to a support coordinator.

The honest headline first. Daniel's Diaries is an educational wellbeing tool, not a therapy service, and it is not a registered NDIS provider. Whether a subscription can be paid from a plan is always a decision for the family and their plan manager against that child's plan, goals, and budget. Nothing in this guide guarantees a claim. What we can do, and what the recommendation letter does, is make the link between the child's disability, their plan goals, and this tool so clear that the decision is easy to make.

Where the subscription fits in a plan

Families and plan managers have typically funded Daniel's Diaries through one of two pathways. Which one applies depends on how the plan is built and how the subscription is being used.

Pathway 1 · Core – Consumables, as low cost assistive technology

A digital subscription that helps a child do something their disability otherwise makes hard can be treated the way plan managers treat other low cost assistive technology: it is low risk, low cost, related to the disability, and it supports the goals in the plan. This is the simplest pathway for Bronze and Silver subscriptions, where the family is using the program at home. The questions a plan manager will ask are the standard ones for any low cost AT purchase: does this relate to the child's disability, does it help the child work toward a plan goal, and is it value for money. The recommendation letter answers all three in writing.

Pathway 2 · Capacity Building – Improved Daily Living

Where the subscription is attached to a skill building goal and recommended by a practitioner as part of how that goal is being worked on, some plan managers fund it from the capacity building budget instead. This framing fits best when Daniel's Diaries is being used alongside your clinical work: the child practises between sessions what you are building within them, and the subscription extends your input rather than duplicating it. If you are supporting the family under a Gold account, this is usually the natural pathway, because the program is explicitly part of your delivery.

In either pathway, the underlying test is the same. The support has to be related to the child's disability, help them pursue the goals in their plan, represent value for money, and not be an ordinary living cost every family would carry anyway. A generic wellbeing app for any child would fail that last test. A structured psychoeducation program recommended by the child's practitioner, targeting the specific skills their disability makes hard, does not. That distinction is the whole job of the recommendation letter.

The arrangement that matters most

Plan management type changes what is possible.

Because Daniel's Diaries is not a registered NDIS provider, the family's plan management arrangement matters more than anything else in this guide.

Plan typeWhat it means for Daniel's Diaries
Self managed The family can purchase from any provider, registered or not. They pay for the subscription, keep the invoice and receipt, and claim reimbursement through the participant portal. This is the most flexible arrangement.
Plan managed The plan manager can pay non-registered providers, so the subscription can be claimed. The family or practitioner sends the plan manager the invoice together with the recommendation letter. This is the most common arrangement among Daniel's Diaries families.
Agency managed The NDIA only pays registered providers, so the subscription cannot be claimed from the plan. The family can self fund it, which many do given the price point, and can raise the support at their next plan review, where your recommendation letter becomes evidence for the review conversation instead.

If you are not sure which arrangement applies, ask the parent one question: who pays your other invoices — you, a plan manager, or the NDIA directly. The answer tells you which row you are in.

The claims environment

What plan managers are looking for in 2026.

The claims environment has tightened this year, and it is worth knowing why a written recommendation now matters more than it used to. Under the integrity reforms rolling out through 2026, plan managers and the NDIA are asking for supporting evidence on claims more often, invoices are being checked more closely, and from 1 December 2026 claims must be lodged within 90 days of the support being delivered rather than the old two year window. None of this is a barrier to a well founded claim. It simply means a claim should arrive with its justification attached rather than hoping nobody asks.

In practice, a plan manager approving a Daniel's Diaries subscription wants to see three things in front of them: an invoice or receipt that clearly identifies the subscription, the tier, and the period it covers; a link to the child's plan goals, which is what your recommendation letter provides; and confidence that the support is disability related rather than a general household expense, which your clinical framing establishes. When those three arrive together, approval is routine. When a subscription invoice arrives on its own, it invites questions.

Encourage families to claim promptly, keep every receipt, and keep a copy of your letter with their plan records. If a claim is ever queried, the letter is the answer to the query.

The template, done well

Using the recommendation letter.

The letter template that accompanies this guide is deliberately short, one page once completed, because plan managers read hundreds of documents and the ones that work are the ones that make the reasonable and necessary link in four tight paragraphs. It follows the same clinical spine you would use in any support justification: the child's impairment, the functional impact of that impairment, and why this particular support addresses that impact. When completing it, hold these principles:

  • Name the functional impact, not just the diagnosis. A diagnosis alone does not justify a support. What justifies it is what the child cannot yet do: recognise rising anxiety before it peaks, recover after a setback, read a peer interaction. Write the letter at that level.
  • Tie it to a goal that is actually in the plan. Quote or closely paraphrase the plan goal. A letter that connects to the child's own stated goals is doing the plan manager's reasoning for them.

  • Describe Daniel's Diaries accurately. It is a structured, evidence informed psychoeducation program that teaches emotional regulation, resilience, and social understanding through guided modules a child works through with adult support. Do not call it therapy, treatment, or intervention, and do not imply it replaces clinical services. Overstating it weakens the claim and misrepresents the tool.
  • Make the value for money case in one sentence. A monthly subscription costs a fraction of a single clinician delivered session and gives the child structured practice every week between sessions. That sentence, adjusted to the child, is usually all the value for money argument a plan manager needs.
The fine print that saves time

Practical notes.

Pricing and tiers

Bronze and Silver are family subscriptions billed monthly. Gold is the practitioner supported tier and is typically arranged through your practice, in which case how it is invoiced to the family follows your own service agreement. Confirm current pricing at danielsdiaries.com.au before quoting figures in a letter, and name the specific tier in both the letter and the invoice so they match.

Invoices and GST

Subscription invoices identify the program, tier, billing period, and ABN. GST treatment of supports purchased under an NDIS plan depends on the family's circumstances, so leave that question with the plan manager rather than advising on it; the invoice shows the GST position either way.

If a claim is declined

It happens, usually because the budget category is exhausted or the plan manager wants a stronger goal link. It is not a judgement on the tool or your recommendation. The family can self fund in the meantime, and the decline is worth raising at the next plan review with your letter attached. If the goal link was the issue, revising the letter to sit closer to the plan's own wording usually resolves it.

Your professional position

Recommending an educational resource sits comfortably within scope for behaviour support practitioners, occupational therapists, psychologists, speech pathologists, and school wellbeing staff. You are not certifying the claim, you are providing a professional opinion on why the tool suits this child. The decision remains with the family and their plan manager, and the letter says so explicitly.

The five minute version for a busy parent

If your child's plan is self managed or plan managed, the subscription can usually be claimed where it clearly supports the goals in the plan. Ask your practitioner for the recommendation letter, send it to your plan manager with the invoice, and keep copies of both. If your plan is agency managed, the subscription cannot come out of the plan for now, but bring the letter to your next plan review.
