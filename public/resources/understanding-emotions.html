Understanding EmotionsFor the grown up
Before you begin

Read this part on your own first.

This resource introduces the second Super Skill in Daniel's Diaries: the Emotion Navigator. It builds straight onto the town your child already knows, and it is worth ten quiet minutes before you sit down together.

Children feel emotions long before they understand them. A big feeling with no frame around it does not feel like information. It feels like the whole world, and everything the child does next comes from inside it. The navigator idea changes that. It tells the child something true and steadying: feelings are not who you are. They are messengers visiting your town, and you are the one who reads what they bring.

What is actually happening in the brain

An emotion begins as signals in the body — a quickening heart, a tight tummy, warmth in the face — and the brain reads those signals, together with what is happening around the child, and makes a feeling out of them. Emotions exist because they are useful: each one carries information and readies the body for action. And critically, they are temporary. A feeling rises, crests, and passes, like a wave.

Messengers, in plain terms

A messenger is an emotion arriving with information. The message is what the feeling is trying to say: anger says something matters, fear says something feels unsafe, sadness says something was lost. The navigator is the child's growing ability to notice the visitor, read the message, and choose what to do next. That capacity has a real name in the research: emotional literacy, one of the strongest foundations of regulation a child can build.

Why messengers, and why a navigator

A messenger is temporary by definition — visitors arrive and visitors leave, which is exactly what feelings do and exactly what a child in a big feeling cannot believe. And it puts the child outside the feeling: a messenger is something you can stand beside and listen to, rather than something the child has to be. Navigating is not suppressing. We are teaching children to read the message and steer.

The part that matters most

There are no bad feelings, and we never tell a child otherwise. Every messenger is allowed into the town — anger, fear, jealousy, all of them. We read the message and stay beside the child. What we guide is what happens next. The feeling is always allowed. The behaviour can still have a limit. A feeling that is allowed is a feeling that can be steered.

A note on timing: this conversation belongs to a calm, ordinary moment, not a hard one. You cannot introduce the messengers while one is pounding on the gate. Lay the idea down on a quiet day.

🌊 Feelings rise, crest, and pass

No messenger moves in. A feeling is a wave: it builds, it peaks, and it always comes back down. Children in a big feeling genuinely believe it will last forever — knowing that visitors leave is half the comfort. The wave does not need fighting, just someone steady nearby while it passes.

📮 Every message is worth reading

Even the uncomfortable messengers are on the child's side. Anger guards what matters. Fear keeps watch. Sadness honours what was lost. When a family reads messages instead of silencing messengers, feelings stop being something to hide and start being something to understand.

Understanding EmotionsSaying it out loud
The conversation

How to introduce it, in your own voice.

This is an offer, not a lesson. Sit beside your child, not across from them. Keep it light, leave room for them to wonder, and stop while they are still interested. Five minutes is plenty.

1
Open a door, gently

Start from the town they already know. Try: "Remember your brain town? I found out something cool about who visits it. Want to hear?" If they say not now, that is fine. The door stays open.

2
Plant the messengers

"Feelings are like messengers who visit your town. Each one knocks on the gate with a message. Angry comes to say something really matters. Scared comes to say check if this is safe. They visit for a while, and then they move on. They always move on."

3
Hand over the job

"And here is the best bit. Every town has a navigator who reads the messages. In your town, that is you. You do not have to do everything a feeling says. You get to read the message, and then choose what happens next."

4
Let them meet a messenger

"Which messenger do you reckon visits your town the most?" The excited one before a party, the worried one at bedtime, the grumpy one when the iPad turns off. Any answer is the right answer. You are just helping them notice that a feeling can be looked at.

✓  Language that opens it up

"I wonder what that feeling came to tell you."

"Every messenger is allowed in your town."

"That one will move on. They always do."

✕  Language that closes it down

"There is nothing to be upset about."

"Stop crying, you are fine."

"Big kids don't get scared of that."

The difference is welcome. The left column lets the messenger in and reads the message together. The right column slams the gate — and a turned away messenger does not leave, it just knocks harder. We are building a navigator who feels capable, not a child who hides their visitors.

Do this together

Meet your messengers.

This is where your child first comes face to face with their feelings — by drawing them as the visitors they are. Grab some paper and whatever they like to draw with; a bigger sheet is even better if they get going.

10–15 minutes No wrong answers Keep the gallery
  • Draw the messengers. Start with four visitors: the angry one, the scared one, the sad one, and the happy one. What do they look like arriving at the town gate? Colours, faces, sizes — entirely up to the artist.
  • Name them. Ask, "What should we call this one?" The Grump, Captain Worry, the Big Blue — their names, their choice. A messenger with a name is far less scary at the gate.
  • Give each one a message. Ask, "What does this one come to tell you?" Write it beside each visitor. Offer the simple ones if they are stuck: angry says something matters, scared says check if this is safe.
  • Spot today's visitor. Before you finish, ask, "Which messenger has visited your town today?" Find them in the gallery together. That one small noticing is the whole skill beginning.

If drawing feelings is hard

Plenty of children, and especially those who find the inside world slippery, do better one step removed. Ask what Daniel's messengers might look like, use a feelings faces chart and point, or choose a colour for each visitor instead of a picture. Meeting a messenger counts every bit as much however it happens.

Understanding EmotionsKeeping it alive
After today

Make the messengers part of the family.

The Emotion Navigator grows through small, ordinary mentions, not big conversations — a home where a feeling arriving is as unremarkable as a knock at the door.

Easy ways to keep it going

  • Name your own visitors out loud: "The frustrated messenger just arrived for me. I am taking a slow breath while I read the message." Children learn the frame by hearing you live it.
  • Welcome the good ones too: "I reckon the proud messenger is visiting you right now." Not every visitor should be a hard one.
  • Notice them leaving too: "That big feeling moved on, hey. They always do."
  • Stay curious, not corrective: "I wonder what that one came to tell you." Wondering keeps the gate open.
Hold this lightly

You will not always know which messenger has arrived, and you do not need to. Guessing warmly, and letting your child put you right, is the skill working. If today plants nothing more than "my feelings are visitors with messages, and I can learn to read them," you have done the whole job.
